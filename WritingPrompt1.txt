	In my eyes, a key method to prevent "information silos"—particularly the partitioning of information and lack of communication that leads to disastrous code merges—is to organize regular meetings with team members in order to assign tasks and exchange occurring problems. With a system like this, team members would be prohibited from making alterations to a section of the codebase that they haven't been assigned to. And if they have their own ideas for that particular section, or would like to work on it for a future assignment, this can be discussed in the meetings and they can receive information from the person or people who worked on it previously. 
	When it comes to evenly distributing workload, I think a person's contribution should be measured by functionality rather than by pure volume of code written. From my (admittedly novice) experience with programming, the make of a good programmer isn't necessarily to perfectly understand all the functions and syntax of a language and be able to mete out large swathes of code. But rather, to be able to logically reason the solution to a problem and then figure out how to use the tools of a language to express that logic in code. With that in mind, the solution to one problem could be very logically complex and yet can be resolved with only a few lines of code; on the other hand, a problem could be very rudimentary yet require many lines of code to implement the solution. Ultimately, programmers spend more time thinking about what to code rather than the actual physical act of writing it, so I think that to be a poor measure of judging contribution.

In another class of mine this semester, I have personally experienced scope creep. In this class, we are assigned into groups of around six people and given four weeks to create a game (we're on the third week of the project right now). With such a limited amount of time to work on the game, it naturally must needs be rather limited in scope. Our initial plan for the game was for it to have multiple areas, each with a unique goal, and our Design Document reflected this. But during the first week we realized that implementing the features of even just one area took way longer than expected (to be completely honest, the programming was mostly finished for that area, it was mainly the art assets that were lagging behind). So, during the team's meeting on the second week, we trimmed our game down to just that one area while making it much more mechanically complex. To be exact, the area is a maze where the original goal was to find and collect a teddy bear; we modified this concept a bit by instead splitting the teddy bear into parts and spreading them throughout the maze. And all the while, a monster roams around and hunts the player down.

This pivot in direction was reflected in our Game Design Document. The areas that we were doing away with as well as the mechanics attached to them were trimmed from the document, and the new mechanics of the singular area added. While the assets to be made by the artists remained mostly unchanged, programmers experienced a large shift in the mechanics they needed to implement. Again, our Design Document reflected this change, we assigned our programmers to their task of choice to make sure that multiple people weren't working on the same mechanic, wasting time and resources, and creating conflicts—both in creative direction, and literal code conflicts.

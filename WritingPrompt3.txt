In the general field of art, there's recently been a trend going around that entails posting an image of your artwork in an early, rough stage, accompanied by the phrase "Make it exist first." and another image showing the finished piece with the phrase "You can make it good later." This is a core philosophy behind the concept of rapid prototyping as it concerns video games; creating a rough version of the game with rudimentary assets and often simplified mechanics. This style of development helps to prevent bottlenecks, as it allows designers and programmers to create the logic and functionality of the game independently of the assets that said functionality will be attached to. For example, in a game's very early or "alpha" stages, the player character might only be a grey capsule, and even though this placeholder lacks any personality, functionality such as moving and jumping can still be programmed and tested while artists simultaneously create the character's model and animations which can then have the movement logic attached to them.

On the contrary, some more advanced features might require a more robust or even fully complete asset to implement or even just to test. A game focused around climbing might use procedural animation (such as inverse kinematics) to figure out the placements of the player character's hands and feet as they climb. In order to test a mechanic such as this, it would require a model with rigged limbs. In some games, this procedural aspect might only be necessary from a visual standpoint, so the underlying mechanics can still be created independently. But other games might have physics-based movements that takes the placement of each limb into account, so the asset is required. This is typically dealt with in the beta stage of the game, where the work of the programmers and artists is combined to create something that will more accurately resemble the final product.

Once everything is in place, a game goes into its polishing phase; and once that's completed it "goes gold" which means it's ready to be released commercially. Aspects of polishing might include tasks such as refining models, textures and animations and almost always includes optimization, play testing and bug fixing. These last few processes might even bleed over into the game's release date—or, at least, close enough to it that the changes can't be certified in time—resulting in the all-too-common day one patches.
